About

I am a biostatistician and statistical analyst with doctoral training in biostatistics and a research background in statistical genetics, complex sampling designs, and health data analysis.

My doctoral research focused on genetic association studies under Extreme Phenotype Sampling, where individuals are selected from the extremes of a trait distribution. Although this design can improve efficiency for detecting genetic effects, it can also make population structure and population stratification especially difficult to account for. If not properly handled, this can lead to inflated false-positive findings in association studies.

A central part of my work was therefore developing statistical methods that could account for population stratification within the extreme phenotype sampling framework. I approached the sampling problem through a missing-data formulation, which allowed information from the sampling design and incomplete genotype data to be incorporated into the analysis rather than treated using standard methods that assume a randomly sampled population.

This work involved mixed-effects models, EM algorithms, missing-data methods, simulation studies, and statistical computing in R. I have since expanded this statistical foundation into broader health and clinical data work using R, SAS, Python, SQL, and Power BI.

Population Structure in Extreme Phenotype Sampling

A major part of my doctoral research investigated how population structure and population stratification affect genetic association studies conducted using Extreme Phenotype Sampling. EPS selectively genotypes individuals from the extremes of a phenotype distribution and can improve efficiency for detecting genetic effects. However, the sampling design can make underlying population structure particularly difficult to account for, increasing the risk of false-positive genetic associations when correction methods are not appropriate for the sampling framework.

I evaluated whether existing mixed-model approaches could adequately control population stratification under EPS. This involved extensive simulation studies examining type I error and statistical power under common- and rare-variant scenarios and comparing methods designed for binary and continuous traits.

The methods evaluated included GMMAT, LEAP, CARAT, and GEMMA. The work showed that performance depended on whether the underlying modelling approach was suitable for the EPS design. Several methods provided appropriate false-positive control for common variants, while other approaches were not suitable for the sampling framework. Rare-variant scenarios remained particularly challenging.

Mixed-Effects Models & Missing Genetic Covariates

Building on this work, I investigated estimation directly within a genetic linear mixed-model framework. I first considered the complete-data setting, using likelihood-based estimation and the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm as a methodological foundation for estimating model parameters and variance components.

I then extended this framework to the incomplete-data setting created by Extreme Phenotype Sampling. In an EPS design, phenotype information can be retained for the full sample while genotypes are collected only for individuals selected from the extremes of the phenotype distribution.

I formulated this selective genotyping design as a missing-data problem. Genotypes belonging to individuals who were not selected for genotyping were treated as missing genetic covariates, while their observed phenotype information remained available for analysis.

This provided a framework for incorporating incomplete genotype information, population structure, and the mixed-model dependence structure within the estimation process rather than discarding information from participants who had not been genotyped.

EM-Based Estimation & Statistical Inference

I developed an EM-based procedure for fitting linear mixed models with missing genetic covariates, motivated by the selective genotyping structure of Extreme Phenotype Sampling.

The procedure used a method of weights adapted to the linear mixed-model setting to handle missing genotype information. I also developed two approaches for testing genetic association: a likelihood-ratio test using importance sampling and a Monte Carlo-based Wald test.

I evaluated the procedure through simulation studies examining parameter estimation, type I error, and statistical power across different missing-data scenarios. The evaluation also identified important limitations. The tests were generally conservative in the simulated settings, statistical power was low, and some parameter estimates showed evidence of bias.

These findings highlighted the computational and inferential challenges of combining population structure, selective genotyping, missing genetic covariates, and mixed-model estimation within a single statistical framework. They also identified areas where further methodological development is needed.

Application to Genetic Epidemiology Data

An important part of this research involved moving beyond simulation to evaluate statistical methods using real genetic epidemiology data. I worked with data from the PROtEUs prostate cancer case-control study conducted in Québec, Canada, a study population with known population substructure.

For the Extreme Phenotype Sampling analyses, individuals from the extremes of the BMI distribution were used to investigate how the mixed-model approaches behaved in the presence of real population structure. This provided an applied comparison with the patterns observed in the simulation studies.

I also applied the EM-based procedure to the PROtEUs data. Although most model parameters could be estimated, the estimated genetic effect was larger than estimates from comparison approaches. Examination of p-values across thousands of genetic markers also suggested remaining false-positive inflation, reinforcing the need for further investigation of the procedure.
